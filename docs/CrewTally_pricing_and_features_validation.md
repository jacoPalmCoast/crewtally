# Evidence backs CrewTally's core, reshapes its pricing

The plan is **partly validated**. The evidence supports the core bet, the free tier's shape, the $7.99 monthly price, Apple in-app purchase under the Small Business Program, the 1099 year-end feature, and both exclusions. It does not support four things. The **$59.99 annual price** sits about $10–$20 above the market. The **Project Pass** has no competitor precedent and faces a real build risk on Replit. **Spanish owner UI** is ranked too low. **Loans, materials, progress photos and Contacts import** have no demand evidence at all. The core bet is sound. Homeowners were the single largest employer group of day laborers (49%) in the only national survey. About half of day laborers reported wage theft, typically "partial payment, rest later". No App Store app serves the homeowner-pays-day-workers niche. That gap is an opening, but it also means nobody has proven that people will pay. The biggest practical finding concerns payments. Replit's only documented purchase path is its built-in RevenueCat integration, and Replit documents it for **monthly and yearly subscriptions only**, not one-time purchases. The Project Pass therefore has to be proven in a hands-on test before it can anchor the pricing. Web checkout is currently legal in the US with **zero Apple commission**, but Replit has no documented Stripe path for mobile apps, and the zero-fee situation may not last. The revised plan below keeps the free tier and the $7.99 monthly price. It cuts the annual plan to $49.99, treats the Project Pass as a test that has to clear a technical gate, moves Spanish owner UI and year-end totals up the list, and puts loans and photos behind interview validation. Where the report touches tax or legal rules, confirm with the IRS pages cited or a tax professional before shipping app copy.

## Homeowners are the top employers of day labor, and no app serves them

The core premise holds up better than most of the plan. The only national day-labor survey (2006, 2,660 workers at 264 hiring sites) found that **employers were homeowners or renters 49% of the time and construction contractors 43%** ([WSWS summary](https://www.wsws.org/en/articles/2006/03/dayl-m10.html)). The survey is 20 years old and counted only visible hiring-site workers. Use it as evidence of *who hires*, not as a market-size figure. The wider market is large. About **26 million owner households (29.7%)** did at least one improvement project in 2023, and **84% of owner spending went to professionals** ([JCHS](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/interactive-item/files/Harvard_JCHS_Improving_Americas_Housing_2025_Key_Facts.pdf)). Among Houzz homeowners, **49% hired specialty trades directly**, compared with about 30% who hired a general contractor ([Houzz](https://www.houzz.com/magazine/2025-u-s-houzz-and-home-renovation-trends-stsetivw-vs~181188659)). Those are homeowners coordinating several workers themselves, which is exactly CrewTally's user. On the crew-lead side, there are **2.9 million nonemployer construction businesses** ([CPWR](https://www.cpwr.com/wp-content/uploads/DataBulletin-April2026.pdf)). No source says what share of homeowners pay by the day rather than by the job, or how many crew leads pay helpers by the day. Those are the two numbers that most need testing in interviews.

The pain is real and matches CrewTally's features closely. The 2006 survey found that nearly half of day laborers had been cheated on wages in the prior two months ([Cornell/NYT summary](https://catherwood.library.cornell.edu/?p=39517)). Worker centers describe the common patterns as **"partial payment with promises of future payment"** and workers **not knowing the contractor's name** ([NDLON](https://ndlon.org/study-day-laborers-vulnerable-to-variety-of-employer-abuses/)). A running balance plus a texted receipt that the worker confirms, with the payer's name on it, addresses both problems directly. NDLON estimates that only about 10% of employers exploit workers. For most homeowners, then, the pain is their own bookkeeping and disputes over how many days were worked, not bad faith. Position the app as "a clear record for both sides", never as protection against wage theft.

The competitive gap is real and cuts both ways. Crew time-clock tools such as Workyard ($6–$13 per user per month) and ClockShark ($51 per month plus per-user fees) assume employees clocking in and a payroll system ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/215558/Workyard/pricing/); [Jibble roundup](https://www.jibble.io/article/time-tracking-software-pricing-plans)). The closest functional match, **Attendance & Salary Tracker**, targets contractors and supervisors, not homeowners, and has too few ratings for the store to show an average ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/attendance-salary-tracker/id6758696537)). Nobody has built a rating base in this niche. The opening is real, and so is the absence of proof that people will pay. The realistic alternative is a spreadsheet or the Notes app, and that is the argument for a generous free tier.

## Pricing holds at $7.99 a month but the annual plan overshoots

**Pro at $7.99/month is validated.** It exactly matches Attendance & Salary Tracker's monthly price. It sits just under the RevenueCat medians of **$9.99/month** for North America and for the Business category ([RevenueCat SOSA 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps); [Business](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2026-business/)).

**Pro at $59.99/year should come down to $49.99.** The North America yearly median is **$39.99**, and the closest competitor charges **$49.99/year** ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/attendance-salary-tracker/id6758696537)). The median Business-category yearly revenue per paying user is only **$35.48** ([RevenueCat Business](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2026-business/)). At $49.99 the annual plan still carries a meaningful discount, about 48% off twelve monthly payments, and it stops being the most expensive plan in a category no one has proven yet.

**The free tier is validated, with one gap to design around.** Tiers named by team size are familiar. The best-rated indie crew app, with 1,800+ ratings, sells "Core Crew", "Workforce" and "Organization" tiers ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/work-hours-tracker-employee/id1397280601)). Because the free substitutes are free, keeping records never-locked and ad-free is the right call. The gap is that a homeowner doing one remodel with three or fewer workers never needs to pay. The worker cap is therefore doing all of the monetization work for the homeowner segment. Houzz's finding that homeowners hire several specialty trades suggests many will go past three workers, but that is an inference to test, not a fact. Set expectations accordingly. Freemium apps convert about **2.1%** of users to paid, against **10.7%** for hard paywalls ([RevenueCat blog](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-trends-benchmarks-2026)). Business apps take a median of **113 days to reach $1K/month**, and only **1.6% ever reach $10K/month** ([RevenueCat Business](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2026-business/)). One lever with good evidence is a longer Pro trial offered when a user hits a limit. Trials of **17–32 days convert at 42.5%**, against 25.5% for trials under 4 days ([RevenueCat blog](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-trends-benchmarks-2026)).

**The Project Pass is unproven on two counts.** On the market side, one-time and lifetime unlocks are common. One in four apps offers a lifetime plan ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)), and the closest competitor sells lifetime access for **$79.99**. But no comparable app sells a pass per project. The logic is good: homeowner usage ends when the remodel ends, and 72% of annual subscribers cancel within the first year ([RevenueCat blog](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-trends-benchmarks-2026)). At $24.99, about three months of Pro, the price is fair for a remodel lasting two to six months. On the build side, the risk is serious and is covered in the next section. Treat the Pass as the homeowner offer and Pro as the crew-lead offer. That split keeps the two from competing for the same buyer.

## Replit supports subscriptions today; the Project Pass needs a hands-on test

**Apple IAP under the Small Business Program is validated, and it is the only documented path.** Replit's Expo setup "only supports modules included in the Expo SDK" and does not support EAS Build ([Replit Docs](https://docs.replit.com/learn/mobile/expo)). Replit does have a first-party **RevenueCat integration**: the Agent installs the packages and writes the subscription logic. It runs in test mode in Expo Go, and "real charges apply only after Apple App Review approval" ([Replit Docs: RevenueCat](https://docs.replit.com/core-concepts/monetization/revenuecat-subscriptions)). RevenueCat is **free until $2,500 in monthly tracked revenue**, then 1% ([The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/replit-revenuecat-help-vibe-coders-monetize/)). The Small Business Program cuts Apple's fee to **15%** for developers under $1M in proceeds. The rate starts **15 days after the end of the fiscal month in which Apple approves enrollment**, so enroll before launch ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/small-business-program/)). Do not install an IAP library by hand. Ask the Agent to "add subscriptions with RevenueCat".

**The Project Pass is the weak link.** Replit's RevenueCat doc lists monthly subscriptions, yearly subscriptions and custom pricing. It does not mention one-time purchases ([Replit Docs: RevenueCat](https://docs.replit.com/core-concepts/monetization/revenuecat-subscriptions)). Apple allows a one-time pass. Auto-renewing subscriptions must "provide ongoing value", and subscriptions may be offered "alongside à la carte offerings" ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). There is also a design choice to settle. The research notes say a pass that can be bought again for each new project behaves like a *consumable* purchase, and Apple does not restore consumables. CrewTally would then have to record which projects are unlocked on its own server, tied to a user account. That description of Apple's purchase types comes from the researcher's background knowledge; Apple's reference page could not be fetched. Before committing, run a short spike. Create the Pass manually in App Store Connect and the RevenueCat dashboard, and check that a Replit-published TestFlight build can buy it and that the purchase survives a reinstall. If that fails, launch with Pro only and add the Pass later.

**Web checkout should be phase 2, not a day-one fallback.** On the US storefront, apps may now include "buttons, external links, or other calls to action" to outside payment methods ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Apple currently collects **zero commission** on those sales ([TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/327527/20260915/app-store-commission-limbo-enters-new-phase-apples-epic-merits-brief-opens-scotus-fight.htm)), so web checkout is not merely a fallback: it is the cheaper channel. The zero-fee situation may not last, though. In August 2026 Apple proposed fees on these outside purchases of up to 15%, including **5% for Small Business Program apps** ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/08/13/apple-proposes-commissions-of-up-to-15-for-off-app-store-purchases-in-the-us/)). The Supreme Court has taken the case, and a ruling is not expected before June 2027 ([TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/327527/20260915/app-store-commission-limbo-enters-new-phase-apples-epic-merits-brief-opens-scotus-fight.htm)). Replit documents no Stripe path for mobile apps ([Replit Docs](https://docs.replit.com/learn/mobile/expo)). Web checkout also needs user accounts and a server that records what each user has bought. Outside the US, anything bought on the web must also be sold as an in-app purchase ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/), 3.1.3(b)). A US app that uses web checkout alone, with no in-app purchase at all, has not been confirmed acceptable to App Review. Build it only after accounts and cloud sync exist. Offer it as a US-only "save on the web" option, and build it so it can be switched off if a fee arrives.

## The $2,000 1099 threshold is confirmed, but it is a crew-lead feature

**The threshold claim is correct.** For payments made in 2026, the 1099-NEC reporting threshold (and the backup-withholding threshold) is **$2,000, up from $600**, and it will be indexed for inflation starting in 2027 ([IRS Instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec); [IRS small-business page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/am-i-required-to-file-a-form-1099-or-other-information-return); [NATP](https://www.natptax.com/news-insights/blog/new-1099-rules-under-obbba-what-tax-pros-need-to-know-for-2026-and-beyond/)). Payments made in **2025** still use **$600**, so the app has to store a threshold for each year rather than one fixed number. Totals should add up payments per worker by calendar year and by the date of payment. The 2026 forms are due **Feb 1, 2027** ([TaxBandits](https://www.taxbandits.com/irs-updates/form-1099-changes-2026/)), which puts a hard date on this feature. Year-end totals need to ship before January.

**The scope needs to change.** The IRS says to report "only when payments are made in the course of your trade or business. Personal payments are not reportable" ([IRS Instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec)). A homeowner paying for work on their own home generally files nothing, so 1099 prompts shown to homeowners are noise at best and misleading at worst. For landlords the answer depends on the facts. Many advisors recommend filing anyway ([Nolo](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/do-landlords-need-to-file-form-1099-nec.html)). The fix is simple. Tag each project as *Personal home*, *Rental* or *Business*, and show 1099 totals only for Rental and Business projects. For homeowners, a more useful notice concerns household employees. If a homeowner controls both what work is done and how it is done, the worker can count as a household employee. Social Security and Medicare then apply once one such worker is paid **$3,000 or more in 2026** ([IRS Pub 926](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p926)). The app should not decide anyone's worker status. It can show a neutral notice when a worker's pay on a Personal project approaches $3,000, with a link to Pub 926, and point users to a tax professional.

**Postpone W-9 capture, meaning storage of Social Security or tax ID numbers.** Holding workers' TINs on a server would make CrewTally responsible for them under state data-breach laws. The FTC Safeguards Rule sets the practical standard: encryption at rest and in transit, and multi-factor authentication ([The Tax Adviser](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2023/may/complying-with-the-safeguards-rule-for-information-security/)). Massachusetts requires encryption of personal information on portable devices ([201 CMR 17.00](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/massachusetts/department-201-CMR/title-201-CMR-17.00)). Ship year-end totals first, with no TIN. If W-9 capture follows, keep the number on the device only (for example in the iOS Keychain), show only the last four digits, and never put it in exports, receipts or texts. Get this design reviewed before it ships.

## Spanish UI earns a promotion; loans, materials and photos await interviews

**Spanish owner UI is validated and should move up.** Hispanic workers make up **34% of the construction workforce**. In the trades most likely to be paid by the day, the shares are **75.2% (drywall), 63.9% (roofing) and 62.5% (painting)**. Hispanic owners run **27.6% of nonemployer construction firms** ([CPWR](https://www.cpwr.com/wp-content/uploads/DataBulletin-December2024.pdf)). Those owners are the crew leads CrewTally wants to charge for Pro. The closest competitor already ships in 17 languages, Spanish included ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/attendance-salary-tracker/id6758696537)). One caveat: CPWR publishes no data on language, so Hispanic ownership does not by itself mean these owners prefer Spanish.

**The receipt growth line is worth keeping, but label it an experiment.** The evidence shows that workers value the record. It does not show that a "Kept with CrewTally" footer brings in new users. Keep the line short and neutral, add a tracking tag to the link so taps and installs can be counted, and judge it on those numbers.

**Loans and advances remain unproven.** Two analog apps include a loan or advance ledger: Attendance & Salary Tracker and HajariBook ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/labour-attendance-register/id1661537437)). Both come from the Indian market, and no US demand data turned up. The legal side is manageable but needs care. Federal wage law lets an employer deduct loan *principal* from pay, but **not interest or fees** where the deduction cuts into minimum wage ([DOL Field Operations Handbook](https://www.dol.gov/whd/foh/foh_ch30.pdf)). If the feature is built, keep the loan balance separate from what the worker has earned, charge no interest, and show earned pay, the deduction and net pay separately on the receipt. Repayments do not reduce what the worker earned for 1099 purposes.

**Materials, progress photos and Contacts import have no evidence.** Materials tracking is plausible for self-funded homeowners: 84% of Houzz renovators paid from savings ([Houzz](https://www.houzz.com/magazine/2025-u-s-houzz-and-home-renovation-trends-stsetivw-vs~181188659)). Free remodel budget apps already exist, though ([Budget My Reno](https://budgetmyreno.com/)). A lightweight version would make the Project Pass worth more, but should not be a headline feature. No source addressed photos. Contacts import is cheap and removes friction, so it can go in whenever there is spare capacity.

**Both exclusions hold.** CrewTally sells digital features, so in-app purchase rules apply. Apple's rule for physical services, 3.1.3(e), does not, because the workers' labor is paid outside the app ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Moving workers' money through the app would raise the open question of whether CrewTally counts as a financial institution under federal privacy rules. The notes flag that question for legal review. GPS clock-in is the core feature of the per-user payroll tools. Leaving it out keeps CrewTally positioned as a tool for the payer, not a surveillance tool.

## Recommended revised plan

| Area | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Free** | 1 active project and up to 3 workers. Unlimited records, never locked. Receipts and receipt links in English and Spanish. No ads. Workers never pay. When a user hits the cap, offer a Pro trial of about 3–4 weeks. |
| **Project Pass** | Test at **$24.99** (and $19.99 if interviews show pushback). Unlocks one project with unlimited workers, materials and export. Build it as a pass the user can buy for each new project, with unlocked projects recorded on CrewTally's server. **Gate:** it must pass a TestFlight purchase-and-restore test on a Replit build first. Otherwise launch without it. |
| **Pro** | **$7.99/month; $49.99/year** (down from $59.99). Aimed at crew leads: unlimited projects, year-end totals, Spanish owner UI and all later features. Consider a lifetime price around $79.99 only after one-time purchases are proven to work on Replit. |
| **Payments** | Launch with **Apple IAP through Replit's built-in RevenueCat integration**. Enroll in the Small Business Program now. Phase 2, after accounts and sync exist: a **US-only web checkout (Stripe)**, offered alongside IAP and never instead of it, with a switch to turn it off if Apple's outside-purchase fee is approved. |
| **Feature order** | (1) Receipt growth line, as a tracked experiment. (2) **Year-end totals per worker**, with a Personal/Rental/Business tag on each project and the $3,000 household-employee notice. No TIN yet, and ship before January 2027. (3) **Spanish owner UI**. (4) Lightweight materials/other costs. (5) Add from Contacts. (6) W-9 capture with on-device storage only, after a security review. (7) Loans/advances, only if interviews show demand. (8) Daily progress photo, only if interviews show demand. |
| **Keep excluded** | Paying workers inside the app; GPS clock-in. |

**Open questions for user validation sessions**

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Homeowners: how many people did you pay on your last project, how were they paid (by day, by hour or by bid), and how long did the project run? | Decides whether the 3-worker cap ever triggers a purchase, and whether the Pass covers a typical project. |
| Would you pay about $25 once for this project, or about $8 a month? At what price does the one-time pass feel wrong? | The Pass has no market precedent, so it needs a direct willingness-to-pay test. |
| How do you track days and payments today (paper, Notes, spreadsheet, Venmo or Zelle memos)? | No source documents current habits. This defines the real competitor. |
| Crew leads: do you issue 1099s, and what do you do at year-end today? Would you enter a helper's tax ID in an app? | Tests demand for year-end totals and how much people trust the app with TINs. |
| Crew leads: do you advance money to helpers and take it back out of later pay? How often, and how much? | The loans feature has no US evidence at all. |
| Do you run your phone in Spanish? Would you use the app in Spanish? | Hispanic ownership does not tell us language preference. |
| Workers: when you get a receipt link, do you open it, keep it or forward it? Would you install the app yourself? | Tests whether the receipt line can actually drive growth. |
| Would you buy materials and log them here, or keep them somewhere else? Would you take a daily progress photo? | These features have no evidence yet. |

## Conclusion

The evidence moves CrewTally's risk away from whether there is a need and toward whether people will pay and whether the purchase setup can be built. The need looks real: homeowners are the largest employer group of day labor, disputes over partial payment are common, and no app serves the niche. Two points remain unproven, and both sit at the center of the plan. One is whether homeowners will pay per project. The other is whether Replit's build pipeline can sell a one-time Pass at all. Both can be settled in the next few weeks, the first with interviews and the second with a single TestFlight test, before any feature work that depends on them.

The research also suggests CrewTally serves two different buyers. Homeowners have a single remodel, a short relationship with the app and a one-time purchase. Crew leads have ongoing work, 1099 deadlines, often Spanish as their working language, and a subscription. Year-end totals, due before February 2027, and Spanish owner UI are what will earn crew leads' subscriptions. The growth loop from receipts can serve both groups, if the measurements show it works.
